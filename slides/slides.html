LagLens: What Does a Robot Do While Its Policy Is Still Thinking?

A visual analytics tool for robot policies that plan in chunks and need time to think

How such a robot works
  1. Plan. A robot policy (a neural network) looks at the camera once and plans its next 16 moves: an action chunk.
  2. Act. The robot performs the first h moves of the chunk without looking again, then needs a new chunk.
  3. Think. Computing a chunk takes time d (inference latency, here 0–400 ms). What should the robot do meanwhile? (right)
    • Sync: wait. Fine while the world waits too, but the robot stands idle, and anything that moves gets away meanwhile.
    • Async: keep moving on the old chunk. No waiting, but the scene changes while the policy thinks, so the new chunk is made for a scene that no longer exists (an outdated plan) and may not continue the current motion: the robot jumps when it switches (a chunk-boundary discontinuity). Naive async just swaps the chunks; inpaint async computes the new chunk so that it continues the old one.
The problem. Given their latency d, engineers pick a strategy and h by comparing one success-rate number per combination, e.g. “d = 4, h = 4: sync 13 % vs. inpaint async 67 %”. That number cannot show which start states failed, when, or why (idling, outdated plans or jumps). We found no visualization tool for this.
Our idea: same start, different schedule. We run a policy from the same 30 start states under 14 schedules (3 strategies × d ∈ {0, 2, 4} × h ∈ {4, 8}) and record every planned chunk, on two tasks: PushT (hard; the block waits while the robot thinks) and Catch the ball (the ball keeps rolling while the robot thinks). Any two schedules can then be compared start by start and replayed move by move.

1 · What — Data Abstraction

Our own recorded runs of two robot policies on two tasks

Same round pusher in both simulated tasks (top view, 512 × 512 px; a move = target position of the pusher). PushT (hard): push a T-shaped block onto a fixed T-shaped goal; pretrained Diffusion Policy lerobot/diffusion_pusht (last 2 camera frames + pusher positions). Catch the ball (ours): touch a ball that rolls and bounces (42–100 px/s, new speed and direction per start state) within 8 s; a small Diffusion Policy we trained on scripted interception demonstrations (pusher and ball positions). Both plan chunks of 16 moves from random noise, so re-running a schedule can change an outcome; a noise replicate measures how often. Per task: 30 start states × (14 schedules + 1 replicate) = 450, in total 900 episodes (recorded runs); every planned chunk and every executed move is logged (47 KB CSV + 21 MB JSON).

Dataset type: multidimensional table of episodes, linked to two temporal tables and a spatial field. Data types: items (episodes, steps, chunks) with attributes; positions (x, y). Step table (131,872 items; key t, ordinal, 10 Hz): pusher, block (PushT) or ball (Catch) and move x, y (interval, px); block θ (cyclic); chunk id (nominal); index j (ordinal, 0–15); age (ratio, steps); held (binary, idle); coverage or distance (ratio). Chunk table (21,689 items): picture time and ready time (ordinal, step), executed window (step range), 16 planned moves (interval, px). Keys (marked KEY): task, strategy, d, h, replicate → schedule; × seed → episode; × t → step; × chunk id → chunk. Values: all attributes without KEY (outcomes, costs, counts; per step positions, move, age, held).
First look: when does acting while thinking pay off?
Sync ● Naive ■ Inpaint ◆ · success rate, real data. In Catch the ball rolls on while sync waits: at d = 4, h = 4 sync 47 %, naive 83 %, inpaint 90 % (inpaint +13 / −0 start states, naive +14 / −3; a re-run flips 4), and async catches sooner (27 vs. 44 steps). In PushT async also wins at d = 4; the tool shows why (next slides).
PushT: Diffusion Policy (Chi et al. 2023), LeRobot checkpoint, gym-pusht · Catch: our task and policy (scripts/catch_task.py); ball size and deadline set so that sync without latency succeeds ≈ 90 % (separate seeds) before any latency was tested2 / 7

3 · Why — Task Abstraction (action + target)

Four tasks, from all schedules down to a single chunk switch

#Action + targetConcrete question (Mara)WhyExpected outcome
T1Compare → trends of success and of each strategy's cost (idle time, outdated plans, jumps) across schedules “How fast does success fall with latency for each strategy and horizon, when the world waits (PushT) and when it moves (Catch)?” Choose a candidate schedule for the latency the lab actually has. A shortlist of robust schedules and a latency budget per strategy.
T2Locate → outliers: initial states whose outcome flips between a baseline and a candidate schedule “Going from sync d=0 to naive async d=4, which seeds start failing, and which are rescued?” Aggregate rates hide which situations break; paired seeds isolate the schedule's effect. A small set of fragile seeds to inspect (and to add to a regression test set).
T3Identify → features in time & space: where idling, outdated plans and jumps occur in a flipped episode “At which step does the candidate diverge from the baseline, and did the robot wait, act on an outdated plan, or jump?” Diagnose the mechanism of failure, not just its rate. A causal hypothesis, e.g. “a 239 px jump knocks the block out of the goal” (PushT) or “sync waits while the ball rolls away” (Catch).
T4Discover → correlation between jumps at chunk switches (boundary discontinuities) and task outcome (coverage, distance to the ball) over paired episodes “When a seed moves from the baseline to the candidate, do larger discontinuities go with worse outcomes? Does inpainting shrink the discontinuities?” If jumps are what breaks episodes, a strategy that shrinks them should rescue seeds; check it on our policy. Evidence for or against a strategy (direction and size of shift per seed).
Arjun mainly needs T1 (latency budget) and T2 (is the drop driven by a few seeds?). Mara chains T1 → T2 → T3 → T4 in one session: overview first, then drill down to a single chunk switch (details on demand). Both run the same tasks on Catch the ball first, where the cost of waiting is obvious, and then on PushT, where the costs of waiting, outdated plans and jumps compete.
Actions: compare · locate · identify · discover — targets: trends · outliers · features · correlation; from overview (T1) to a single chunk switch (T3)4 / 7
4 · How — Vis Idiom Design (4 linked views, overview → filter → details)

Each view answers one task with the most effective idiom

ViewTaskIdiom & why (VIS literature)Interaction
A · Latency ResponseT1
“How fast does each strategy get worse as the policy gets slower?”
Line chart small multiples: one panel per horizon h, x = latency d on its true scale, y = metric, strategies superposed (hue + shape). Position on a common scale beats the luminance of a heatmap [Cleveland & McGill 84; Heer & Bostock 10]; superposition for direct sync-vs-async comparison, juxtaposition across h [Gleicher 11]. 95 % interval lines instead of bars [Correll & Gleicher 14]; hover = paired difference to the baseline.		Click mark = candidate, shift-click = baseline; one question bar: Success · Idle (cost of sync) · Outdated (cost of async) · Discontinuity (cost of naive async), drives A and B; recomputes on seeds selected in B.
B · Paired Seed TableT2, T4
“Which start states flip between baseline and candidate, and is that more than chance?”
Composite Table-Lens view [Rao & Card 94; Javed & Elmqvist 12]: one row per seed shared by (left) an outcome matrix — filled = success, baseline column black, candidate column orange — and (right) dumbbells baseline → candidate of the attribute that belongs to A's question. Rows are grouped by baseline→candidate change (spatial region), so flips break their band's pattern; a re-run column shows chance flips (PushT 8/30).		No controls of its own. Click column header = candidate; click cell = open episode; brush dumbbells or click a band → cross-filter A [Weaver 10].
C · Workspace ReplayT3
“Watch both runs side by side: what was planned, what was done, where it jumped.”
Juxtaposed baseline | candidate maps at identical scale (true geometry). Overlays: current chunk, executed (solid) and thrown away (dashed); next chunk being computed (dotted); latest discontinuity (magenta); what the policy saw (dotted outline of block or ball & pusher in the chunk's picture, arrow to the robot now: the gap = how outdated the plan is).		Play / scrub / ← → (navigate t); hover a planned point → when planned / executed; click → jump.
D · Execution TimelineT3
“When was the policy thinking, which plan did each move come from, where were the jumps?”
Gantt lanes on a shared linear time axis [Plaisant 96; Brehmer 17]: per run a think track (inference) and an act track (chunk spans, hatched = idle), a motion track (movement per step, jumps as magenta bars on the same scale), coverage (PushT) or ball distance (Catch) below. Sync shows think/act alternating, async overlapping. The chunk at the cursor is traced picture ○ → think → act, with a 16-cell strip of its planned moves: skipped while thinking · executed · discarded.		Brush a time window → highlighted path in view C; hover chunk → its plan in view C; click → cursor.
Why this is not a generic dashboard: C and D trace every executed move back to the plan (chunk) and the camera frame it came from, which no chart tool offers; A and B compare two schedules start state by start state against a chance baseline (re-run), not just averages. Two tasks, one tool: a task switch in the header shows the same four views for Catch the ball, where async's advantage is plain to see (sync stands still while the ball rolls away), next to PushT, where the costs compete. Less is more: the first draft had 5 views and ~10 hues; following parsimony and consistency [Wang Baldonado 00] we merged two views, gave every hue one meaning and cut ten controls to one question bar.
Shneiderman's mantra (1996): overview first (A), zoom & filter (B), details on demand (C, D) · interactions after Heer & Shneiderman (2012)5 / 7
