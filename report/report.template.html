LagLens: Seeing What a Robot Actually Executes When Its Policy Thinks Too Slowly

Theme: VIS + AI  ·  Live demo: https://joowan1108.github.io/laglens/  ·  Source: github.com/joowan1108/laglens (src/; run locally: python3 -m http.server 8000 --directory src)
Modern visuomotor and VLA policies predict chunks of future actions and execute part of each chunk open-loop. Because inference takes time, the robot either waits (synchronous) or keeps executing an old chunk and switches when a new one arrives (asynchronous). Researchers tune the strategy, the latency budget and the execution horizon from aggregate success curves. LagLens replays one pretrained policy from the same {{N_SEEDS}} initial states under {{N_SCHED}} execution schedules (plus a noise replicate), logs every predicted chunk, and links five d3.js views so that an analyst can go from “which schedule is robust?” down to “which chunk hand-over pushed the block away?”.

1 · What: Data Abstraction

Source. We generated the data ourselves (no public dataset logs per-inference chunks). The pretrained lerobot/diffusion_pusht Diffusion Policy [2] (chunk length 16, 2 observation frames) runs in the PushT simulator at 10 Hz (1 step = 100 ms) on CPU with a 10-step DDIM sampler. A schedule = strategy × latency d × execution horizon h: sync (hold still for d steps, then execute h actions), naive async (keep executing the old chunk while inference runs, switch after d steps and skip the d outdated actions), and inpaint async (as naive, but the new chunk is sampled with the committed actions clamped and the old plan softly blended in over the overlap; a training-free approximation of Real-Time Chunking [8]). Async schedules with d = 0 equal sync d = 0, and d > h is infeasible (two inferences would overlap), giving 46 schedules. A replicate of sync d0 h8 with different sampling noise measures chance flips.

Dataset type: a multidimensional table of episodes ({{N_EP}} items × {{N_ATTR}} attributes, {{CSV_KB}} KB), each linked to two temporal tables, steps ({{N_STEPS}} items) and chunks ({{N_CHUNKS}} items, each with 16 predicted 2-D actions), and to a 2-D spatial field (512 × 512 px workspace); {{DETAIL_MB}} MB of JSON lazy-loaded per schedule. Keys: strategy, latency, horizon, replicate (→ schedule), seed (→ episode), t (→ step), chunk id (→ chunk). Everything else is a value.

5 · Implementation

  • Interface (src/): plain ES modules + d3.js v7.9.0 only (bundled, no other charting library). One shared state object with a d3.dispatch bus; each view re-renders only for the state keys it depends on. Per-schedule logs (≤ 5.3 MB) are fetched lazily and indexed once (executed index set per chunk), so scrubbing and hovering stay interactive. URL parameters reproduce any view state (used for every figure here).
  • Data pipeline (scripts/): generate_rollouts.py runs all seeds of a schedule in lockstep so that one batched diffusion call serves all 50 environments (CPU, ≈ 2–10 min per schedule); build_dataset.py derives metrics; verify_dataset.py checks the ≥ 100 items / ≥ 6 attributes requirement.
  • Testing: ui_test.py loads the app in headless Chromium, runs {{N_UI_CHECKS}} interaction checks across all views, and fails on any console error (all pass, 0 errors). Two generator bugs found during analysis (an off-by-one inpainting mask and a dropped chunk when d = h) were fixed and the affected data regenerated.

6 · Case Study: Mara chooses an execution schedule for 400 ms of latency

Mara's server adds ≈ 400 ms (d = 4). She opens LagLens with R = sync d0 h8, the policy's default.

Finding 1 (T1): at 400 ms, inpainting is the only strategy that stays flat across horizons. In A (Fig. 3) every cell is orange: no schedule beats the zero-latency baseline (72 %). Reading the d4 column, sync drops to 44 / 26 / 14 / 0 % for h = 8 / 6 / 4 / 2 (idle share up to 67 %), naive async to 62 / 56 / 38 %, while inpaint async holds 60 / 58 / 60 %. Switching the colour to seam gap shows why naive degrades at short horizons: its mean seam grows to 43.5 px at d4 h4 and 94.2 px at d2 h2, while inpainting stays ≤ 20.6 px everywhere. The horizon also matters without latency: sync d0 succeeds 72 % at h8 but only 28 % at h4, the jittery re-planning Chi et al. reported [2].
Finding 2 (T2): about one in five outcomes flips by chance, so small differences are noise. Setting C to the noise replicate (Fig. 4) gives 6 losses and 5 gains: 11 of 50 seeds (22 %) flip with an identical schedule. Schedules such as naive d1 h8 (9 losses / 7 gains) or sync d2 h8 (7 / 6) are therefore indistinguishable from the baseline, whereas sync d4 h8 (19 / 5) is a real loss. Sorting by robustness exposes fragile seeds: seed 1001 succeeds under R but in only 15 % of all schedules, so Mara removes such seeds from her claims.
Finding 3 (T3): naive async fails through “mode ping-pong” at chunk seams, not through staleness. For seed 1035 both schedules have the same staleness (4–7 steps), and naive async is even ahead until t ≈ 105 (coverage 86 % vs. 66 %). In D, C's seam ticks then explode (184 px at t = 112, 259 px at t = 116, 190–237 px afterwards), while R's stay below 21 px. Scrubbing C to t = 116 shows the cause: the old chunk (planned from the observation at t = 108) heads to the top of the block, the new chunk (planned at t = 112, after the agent had moved down) starts at the bottom and turns left. The agent swings across the T, which slides out of the goal: coverage falls from 73 % (t = 112) to 0 % (t = 118). Inpainting keeps the committed tail of the old plan, so R's fans continue the same motion and the episode finishes at t = 204.
Finding 4 (T4): naive-async failures come with large seams; inpainting shrinks every seam but not every failure. With R = naive d4 h4 and C = inpaint d4 h4, E (Fig. 6) groups the 50 seeds by outcome change. The black ▲ medians separate the groups: seeds that failed under naive async have median max seams of 221–224 px, seeds that succeeded 86–114 px. All 50 dumbbells point left: inpainting lowers the largest seam of every seed, to at most 52 px. 16 seeds are rescued and 5 lost (5 ≤ 11, within the noise floor of Finding 2). Yet 15 seeds still fail under inpainting although their seams (median 32 px) are as small as in successful runs (21–28 px), so their failures need another explanation (e.g. reduced reactivity), which Mara inspects next with C/D. Over all schedules, failed naive episodes have a median max seam of 81 px vs. 50 px for successes, consistent with [12]; for inpainting 23 vs. 20 px.

Outcome. Mara proposes inpaint async with h = 4–8 for d ≈ 4, reports differences only beyond the 11-flip noise floor, and gives Arjun a latency budget: sync is acceptable only up to d = 2 at h = 8.

7 · Limitations

One simulated task and one policy; latency is quantised to 100 ms steps; the inpainting strategy is a training-free approximation of RTC; a 10-step DDIM sampler replaces the 100-step DDPM sampler for CPU feasibility (the replicate shows that this changes outcomes of individual seeds but not the 70–72 % baseline rate). With 50 seeds, success differences below ≈ 11 flips should not be interpreted.
