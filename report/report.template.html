LagLens: Seeing What a Robot Actually Executes When Its Policy Thinks Too Slowly

Interface: LagLens  ·  Theme: VIS + AI  ·  Live demo: https://joowan1108.github.io/laglens/  ·  Source: github.com/joowan1108/laglens (src/; run locally: python3 -m http.server 8000 --directory src)
Modern robot policies (neural networks that map camera images to motor commands) predict a chunk of future actions from one observation and execute its first h actions open-loop, without looking at the scene again (h = execution horizon). Because a chunk is ready only some time d after the picture it was planned from (latency), the robot either waits (synchronous) or keeps executing the old chunk and switches when the new one arrives (asynchronous). Researchers choose the strategy and h for their latency from aggregate success curves. LagLens replays a policy from the same {{N_SEEDS}} start states under {{N_SCHED}} execution schedules, each run three times, on two tasks (PushT, where the block waits, and Catch the ball, where the ball keeps rolling while the robot thinks), logs every predicted chunk, and links five d3.js views so that an analyst can go from “which schedule is better at my latency, beyond chance?” down to “which chunk switch pushed the block away?”.

1 · What: Data Abstraction

Source. We generated the data ourselves (no public dataset logs per-inference chunks). In the simulated PushT task a round pusher must push a T-shaped block onto a fixed T-shaped goal. The pretrained lerobot/diffusion_pusht Diffusion Policy turns the last 2 camera frames into a chunk of 16 actions (2-D target positions) by removing noise from a random start (10 DDIM steps, CPU, 10 Hz: 1 step = 100 ms), so outcomes are stochastic. Latency d = the delay between the camera picture and the moment its chunk is ready, whatever its cause (computing a large model, transfer to and from a server); it is simulated as a constant number of 100 ms steps, not measured. A schedule = strategy × latency d × horizon h: sync (hold still for d steps, then execute h actions), naive async (keep executing the old chunk while the new one is computed, then switch and skip its d outdated actions) and inpaint async (as naive, but the new chunk is generated with the already committed actions held fixed, so it continues the old plan; a training-free approximation of Real-Time Chunking). We use d ∈ {0, 2, 4} × h ∈ {4, 8, 11}: {{N_SCHED}} schedules (async at d = 0 equals sync). h = 11 is the longest horizon that fits the 16-action chunk at every latency (1 + d + h ≤ 16). Every schedule is run three times from the same start states (run 0 and two re-runs with other sampling noise), so the interface can show how often an outcome changes by chance. Start states are the first 30 seeds (1000–1029, fixed before looking at outcomes); a first grid of 46 schedules × 50 seeds was too dense to read and is kept only as raw logs. Catch the ball (ours) is a second task in which the world keeps moving: the same pusher must touch a ball (radius 25 px) that rolls at {{BALL_SPEED}} px/s and bounces off the walls, within 80 steps (8 s). Its policy is a small Diffusion Policy (MLP denoiser, the same 16-action chunks) that we trained in 4 min on CPU from 4,000 scripted interception demonstrations; ball size and deadline were calibrated on separate seeds (5000–5099) before any schedule with latency was run. Both tasks share seeds and schedules.

Dataset type: a multidimensional table of episodes ({{N_EP}} items over both tasks × {{N_ATTR}} attributes), each linked to two temporal tables, steps ({{N_STEPS}} items) and chunks ({{N_CHUNKS}} items, each with 16 predicted 2-D actions), and to a 2-D spatial field (512 × 512 px workspace); {{DETAIL_MB}} MB of JSON, lazy-loaded per schedule. Keys: task, strategy, latency, horizon, replicate (→ schedule), seed (→ episode), t (→ step), chunk id (→ chunk). Everything else is a value.

Derived metrics. Jump: at every switch from chunk k to k+1, the distance between the first action executed from k+1 and the action k had planned next. Outdated plan: for every executed chunk, how far the robot moved between the camera picture the chunk was planned from and the step it was ready. Idle: share of steps in which the robot holds still while the policy thinks (≈ d/(h+d) for sync). Step values: pusher x, y and block x, y, θ (PushT) or ball x, y (Catch), action, chunk id, index in the chunk, age of its picture, held (idle step), coverage or distance.

4 · How: Vis Idiom Design

Design goal: make the cost of each strategy visible, and separate it from chance. Sync pays with time (idle {{DG_IDLE}} % of the steps at d4 h4 in PushT), both async strategies with outdated plans (the robot moved {{DG_TM_NAIVE}} px (naive) and {{DG_TM_INPAINT}} px (inpaint) between the picture and the plan, vs. {{DG_TM_SYNC}} px for sync), naive async also with jumps (mean {{DG_J_NAIVE}} px per switch vs. {{DG_J_INPAINT}} px for inpaint). The header asks one question for A, B and C (show: Success · Idle · Outdated plan · Jump) and switches the task. Because one run of 30 start states is noisy, every schedule was run three times; the re-runs appear as hollow marks in A and B and as narrow cells in C, so the analyst sees the size of chance next to every difference. Comparison rule: of the {{P_NPAIRS}} pairs of the {{N_SCHED}} schedules, A compares the {{P_NCMP}} that have one reading: the same latency (a choice the analyst can make), the same strategy and h at two latencies (what more latency costs), and any schedule against d = 0 at the same h. The other {{P_NOTCMP}} differ in latency and in strategy or h, so a difference has two possible causes; they are hatched and cannot be chosen. Iteration: earlier versions (header drop-downs for the pair, tabs, a 15-schedule outcome matrix, more hues) were judged too complex or invited trying every combination; the pair is now chosen only from what A shows (rationale: docs/design_rationale.md).

5 · Implementation

  • Interface (src/): plain ES modules + d3.js v7.9.0 only. One shared state object with a d3.dispatch bus; each view re-renders only for the state keys it depends on. Per-schedule logs are fetched lazily and indexed once, so scrubbing stays interactive. URL parameters reproduce any view state (used for every figure here).
  • Data pipeline (scripts/): generate_rollouts.py runs all seeds of a schedule in lockstep with one batched diffusion call (CPU, ≈ 2 min per schedule); generate_catch.py trains and runs the Catch policy; build_dataset.py derives the metrics; verify_dataset.py checks the ≥ 100 items / ≥ 6 attributes requirement. Every result quoted in the case study is computed from the data files by build_report.py.
  • Testing: ui_test.py runs {{N_UI_CHECKS}} interaction checks in headless Chromium and fails on any console error (all pass). It recomputes the framed cells of A independently from episodes.csv (exact McNemar + Benjamini–Hochberg) and compares them with the interface.

6 · Case Study: Mara chooses an execution schedule for 400 ms of latency

Mara's policy needs ≈ 400 ms per chunk (d = 4). She opens LagLens on PushT; A opens at d = 4, the latency she has.

Finding 1 (T1): in PushT, waiting only hurts when the horizon is short. The ranking strip (Fig. 3) puts sync h4 last ({{P_M_SYNC_4}} %, mean of three runs), and its row in A has dark frames in {{P_ROW_SIG}} of its {{P_ROW_N}} compared cells: inpaint d4 h4 rescues {{P_G_SI4}} start states and loses {{P_L_SI4}}. With a longer h, sync stands still less often (idle {{P_IDLE_4}} → {{P_IDLE_8}} → {{P_IDLE_11}} % for h = 4, 8, 11) and the strategies converge (Fig. 4): at h = 11, sync, naive and inpaint reach {{P_M_SYNC_11}}, {{P_M_NAIVE_11}} and {{P_M_INPAINT_11}} %. All {{P_NSIG}} framed pairs of PushT contain an h = 4 schedule; no two schedules with h ≥ 8 differ beyond chance. The horizon matters even without latency: sync d0 succeeds in {{P_D0R0_4}} % of the start states at h = 4 but {{P_D0R0_8}} % at h = 8 and {{P_D0R0_11}} % at h = 11 (run 0).
Finding 2 (T1, T2): when the world keeps moving, acting while thinking wins at every horizon. After the task switch to Catch the ball, sync at d = 4 stays at {{C_SYNC4_RANGE}} % for every h while the async schedules reach {{C_ASYNC4_RANGE}} % (Fig. 4, right). In A, sync at d = 4 is the worse side of all {{C_NSIG}} framed pairs. At h = 11, C shows naive rescuing {{C_G_NAIVE_11}} start states and losing {{C_L_NAIVE_11}}, inpaint {{C_G_INPAINT_11}} and {{C_L_INPAINT_11}}. Start state 1010 (Fig. 5) shows why: sync waits 400 ms before every chunk, the ball rolls on, and each plan aims where the ball was (dotted circle); at t = 20 sync is still {{S10_DS}} px from the ball and inpaint {{S10_DI}} px; inpaint touches it at t = {{S10_TI}}, like sync without latency (t = {{S10_T0}}), while sync never does. Naive async pays with jumps: at d4 h4 its largest jump per episode has a median of {{C_NJ_MED}} px (up to {{C_NJ_MAX}} px between planned targets), inpaint at most {{C_IJ_MAX}} px.
Finding 3 (T2): an equal success rate is not equal behaviour, and C shows which part is chance. In PushT at h = 11, sync → inpaint rescues {{P_G_INPAINT_11}} start states and loses {{P_L_INPAINT_11}}: the rates are close, but {{P_G_INPAINT_11}} + {{P_L_INPAINT_11}} start states change outcome. C puts this next to chance: its two re-runs change {{P_RR_SYNC_11}} start states, and {{P_SOLID_11}} of the {{P_FL_11}} flips come back in all three runs of both schedules, so these flips are noise, not a strategy effect. Over all schedules a plain re-run flips {{P_RR_MIN}}–{{P_RR_MAX}} of 30 PushT start states (median {{P_RR_MED}}) and {{C_RR_MIN}}–{{C_RR_MAX}} in Catch (median {{C_RR_MED}}), which is why A tests and frames instead of showing raw differences. In Catch the same pair is {{C_G_INPAINT_11}} rescued against {{C_L_INPAINT_11}} lost; only {{C_SOLID_11}} of those flips repeats in all runs, so the evidence is the consistent direction, not individual start states.
Finding 4 (T3): naive async fails through “mode ping-pong” at chunk switches. For start state 1017 both schedules plan from equally old pictures (4–7 steps) and naive async moves somewhat more while thinking (mean {{S17_TM_N}} vs. {{S17_TM_I}} px); until t ≈ 150 both hold the block about {{S17_C150}} % in the goal. In E's jump track the candidate's bars then grow ({{S17_J140}} px at t = 140, {{S17_J156}} px at t = 156) and peak at {{S17_J160}} px at t = 160, while the baseline's stay ≤ {{S17_JI_MAX}} px in the window. Scrubbing D to t = 160 shows the jump: the old chunk planned its next move on one side of the block, the new chunk starts at the other, and the card reads “robot moved {{S17_MV_N}} px since then” (baseline: {{S17_MV_I}} px). The pusher swings across the T, which slides out of the goal: coverage falls from {{S17_C150}} % (t = 150) to {{S17_C160}} % (t = 160) and {{S17_C168}} % (t = 168) and recovers only to {{S17_CEND}} %, under the 95 % threshold. Inpainting keeps the committed part of the old plan, so the baseline's moves continue and it finishes at t = {{S17_FIN_I}}.
Finding 5 (T4): naive-async failures come with larger jumps; inpainting shrinks every jump but not every failure. With naive d4 h4 as baseline and inpaint d4 h4 as candidate, C (Fig. 7) shows {{F5_G}} start states rescued and {{F5_L}} lost. Start states that fail under naive async have median jumps of {{F5_NFAIL}} px, those that succeed {{F5_NSUCC}} px. In {{F5_ALL_LEFT}} 30 rows the dumbbell points left: inpainting lowers the mean jump of every start state, to at most {{F5_I_MAX}} px. Yet {{F5_BOTHFAIL_N}} start states still fail under inpainting with median jumps of {{F5_BOTHFAIL_I}} px, close to its successes ({{F5_ISUCC}} px), so their failures need another explanation, which Mara inspects in D and E. Over all latencies, failed naive episodes have a median jump of {{F5_NAIVE_FAIL}} px vs. {{F5_NAIVE_SUCC}} px for successes; for inpainting {{F5_INPAINT_FAIL}} vs. {{F5_INPAINT_SUCC}} px.

Outcome. For PushT at d ≈ 4, Mara avoids sync with a short horizon; with h ≥ 8 the strategy makes no difference she can show beyond chance, and she reports only differences that A frames. For moving objects (Catch) she recommends async at any horizon, and inpainting where jumps matter. For Arjun's budget she sets A to d = 2: in PushT its framed pairs again all involve h = 4; in Catch, sync is lower ({{C_SYNC2_RANGE}} % vs. {{C_ASYNC2_RANGE}} % for async) but no pair is framed, so with 30 start states a 200 ms budget shows no established loss, while 400 ms does. She gives him the re-run flips as the chance level to expect.

7 · Limitations

Two simulated tasks; the Catch policy is small and trained on scripted demonstrations, and the ball ignores contact, so the task isolates the cost of acting late in a moving world rather than modelling a real application. Latency is constant within a run and quantised to 100 ms steps: varying delays (network jitter, or a delay longer than Arjun's budget) are not modelled, and the three values of d show the effect of the latency level only. h ≤ 11 because a chunk has 16 actions. The inpainting strategy is a training-free approximation of RTC; a 10-step DDIM schedule replaces the original 100-step one for CPU feasibility. Tests compare run 0 of each schedule (the re-runs serve as the chance yardstick); with 30 start states, small differences cannot be established, as the {{P_UNFRAMED}} of {{P_NCMP}} compared PushT pairs without a frame show. Schedules share the start states but not the sampling noise (the random stream depends on the schedule and the batch), so every paired difference contains sampling noise; the re-runs show how much.
